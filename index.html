📜 A short history of Nottingham

The place of caves. The earliest name recorded for the settlement is the Welsh Tigguocobauc, "the place of caves", because people were already carving homes into the soft sandstone cliffs. In the 6th century an Anglo-Saxon chief called Snot settled here, and the town became Snotingaham, the homestead of Snot's people. The S was dropped after the Normans arrived.

Castle and outlaws. William the Conqueror built a wooden castle on Castle Rock in 1068, and it grew into one of the great royal fortresses of medieval England. Richard I besieged it in 1194, and in 1330 the young Edward III and his friends crept up a secret passage through the rock, now called Mortimer's Hole, to seize Roger Mortimer. The Robin Hood ballads, with their wicked Sheriff of Nottingham, date from this era.

Civil war and a ducal palace. Charles I raised his standard here in 1642 at the start of the Civil War, but the town and castle were held for Parliament, and the castle was pulled down afterwards. The Duke of Newcastle built the mansion you see today on the Rock in the 1670s. Rioters protesting against the Reform Bill burned it in 1831, and it stood as a shell until it reopened in 1878 as one of the first municipal art galleries outside London.

Queen of the Midlands. From the 1700s Nottingham was a centre of framework knitting and then machine-made lace. The Luddites began smashing knitting frames near here in 1811. By the late 1800s the Lace Market's tall red-brick warehouses were showing Nottingham lace to buyers from around the world, while the poor crowded into some of England's worst slums, many living in cellars and caves.

Bikes, pills and cigarettes. Jesse Boot opened his chemist's shop on Goose Gate in 1849, and it grew into Boots. Raleigh bicycles and John Player's cigarettes made the city a manufacturing powerhouse. The Council House on the Old Market Square opened in 1929, and the stone lions in front became the city's favourite meeting point.

Writers and makers. D. H. Lawrence grew up in nearby Eastwood, and Lord Byron's ancestral home, Newstead Abbey, lies just north of the city. Alan Sillitoe's Saturday Night and Sunday Morning is set in the city's backstreets. Paul Smith opened his first tiny shop here in 1970.

Today Nottingham is a big student city with two universities, and it was named a UNESCO City of Literature in 2015. The Lace Market and Hockley's warehouses are now full of independent shops, bars and studios, and Sneinton Market's old wholesale units house the city's makers.

🕳️ A city built on caves

Nottingham sits on soft Sherwood sandstone that can be carved with hand tools, and for a thousand years people have dug into it. More than 800 man-made caves lie under the city: tannery pits, cellars, malt kilns, wartime air-raid shelters and the backs of pubs.

Pubs in the rock. Ale keeps beautifully at a steady cave temperature, so many of the city's inns stored their beer in cellars cut into the rock, and some of their drinking rooms are caves too. Several still have cave rooms you can drink in.

See them properly. The City of Caves tour takes you through medieval tannery and Victorian slum caves, and the Castle runs tours of Mortimer's Hole and the other passages under the Rock.

🍺 The rock-pub trail

A walk of about 2 km that you can do in an afternoon, roughly in walking order. Each stop is in the guide, so tap a name to see it.

Pace yourself with halves. Most of these pubs serve food, and the Trip is busiest on summer weekends.

🍻 Ordering like a local

  • Castle Rock and Blue Monkey are the local breweries. Harvest Pale is Castle Rock's best-known beer.
  • Ask for "a pint of the local" and the bar staff will point you at what's on.
  • Real ale is served from the cask with a handpump, slightly cool and gently carbonated. It's not flat, it's meant to be like that.
  • A cob is a bread roll. Ask for a cob, not a bap or a barm.
